Dripping Bathtub Faucet Repair in Stratford, CT: Why It Drips and How It Is Fixed

A tub faucet that keeps dripping after it is shut off has a worn part inside. The type of faucet decides the part, and the part decides the repair.

Written by the Stratford 24/7 Plumbing team.

First, which kind of faucet do you have.

Tub faucets come in four main types. A compression faucet has separate hot and cold handles that you turn several times. A cartridge faucet has one or two handles that move smoothly. A ball faucet is common in kitchens but rare in tubs, and a ceramic disc faucet uses a seal made from hard ceramic. Each has different parts and failure patterns.

You often can tell by the handle. Two handles that screw down are usually compression. A single handle that pivots and slides is usually a cartridge. If you are unsure, a plumber can identify it in seconds when the handle is off.

Faucet type, usual failure and repair.

Causes.

Why a tub faucet drips.

Worn washers or O rings.

Rubber hardens with age and stops sealing.

A corroded valve seat.

The surface that the washer presses on becomes rough.

A failed cartridge.

The internal part no longer closes fully.

Mineral buildup.

Deposits keep parts from seating properly.

High water pressure.

Excess pressure pushes past marginal seals.

A leaking diverter.

Water drips from the spout after you start the shower.

Drips add up.

A slow drip may seem harmless, but it wastes water around the clock, stains the tub and can raise your bill. Hot water drips waste energy as well, since the water heater has to keep making more. Fixing a drip early costs less than letting it get worse, because the leaking water wears the seat and makes it harder to seal later.

If a handle is hard to turn, drips from behind the wall or leaves water stains on the ceiling below, the problem may be more than a worn washer. That is a reason to have it looked at promptly.

How the repair goes.

1.

Shut off the water.

We close the supply stops or the main so no water reaches the faucet.

2.

Remove the handle and trim.

The handle, escutcheon and bonnet or cartridge are taken off.

3.

Replace worn parts.

Washers, seals or the cartridge are replaced with matching parts.

4.

Reassemble and test.

We check hot and cold, the shower diverter and any remaining drips.

Do it yourself or call a plumber.

If the tub has no separate shutoff, you may need to close the main. A plumber can add stops during the repair.

Reasonable to try.

  • Replacing a washer in a compression faucet.
  • Cleaning mineral buildup from the aerator or spout.
  • Replacing a cartridge when you know the exact model.
  • Tightening a loose handle screw.

Call a plumber for.

  • No shutoff valve for the tub.
  • A faucet you cannot identify.
  • Corroded or broken parts.
  • Leaks inside the wall.

What you will notice after the repair.

A properly repaired tub faucet shuts off with a firm stop and no drip at the spout. The handle feels smooth, and hot and cold water arrive without sputtering. If the shower diverter was part of the problem, water should go to the shower head only, with nothing coming from the tub spout.

Run the water for a minute at each setting and watch the spout for a few minutes afterward. A tiny drip right after shutoff is normal as residual water drains. A continuing drip means a part is still not seating, and it is worth a second look rather than a long wait.

Local note.

Stratford note: soft water and faucet parts.

Aquarion's 2025 report for the Eastern Fairfield County system, which serves Stratford, lists average hardness at about 3 grains per gallon. With soft water, mineral crust on faucet parts is less common than in hard water areas, so worn rubber and aging cartridges are the more likely culprits.

Homes in Shelton and Trumbull drawing on the same system see similar wear patterns, so age is usually the better guide.

When a drip points to a bigger issue.

If several faucets in the house drip at once, check your water pressure. A failed pressure reducing valve can push more pressure through fixtures than they were designed to handle. If a tub faucet is old, corroded and no longer sold in its original style, replacement can be a better fix than hunting for parts.

A good plumber will tell you which route is better for your faucet and explain the options in plain words.
