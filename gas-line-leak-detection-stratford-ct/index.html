Gas Line Leak Detection in Stratford, CT: Safety Steps First

A gas leak is not a plumbing problem to troubleshoot from inside the house. If you smell gas, leave first. This guide explains the safe order of steps and what happens after you are outside.

Act now.

If you smell gas, do this before reading further.

Leave the building right away and take everyone with you. Do not turn lights or appliances on or off, do not use a phone, lighter or doorbell inside and do not try to find the source. Sparks from a switch can ignite gas.

From a safe distance outside, call 911 and your gas utility's emergency line. Stay out until emergency crews say it is safe. A plumber can help afterward, but only once the immediate danger is handled.

How to recognize a gas leak.

Natural gas is odorless, so utilities add a smell often compared to rotten eggs. That smell is your first warning. Other signs include a hissing sound near a pipe or appliance, dead patches of grass over a buried line, bubbles in standing water and a pilot light that keeps going out.

Physical symptoms such as headache, dizziness, nausea or fatigue indoors can also signal a problem, including carbon monoxide from a poorly burning appliance. Leave the building if you feel these, and get medical help if they are severe.

Common sources.

Where gas leaks tend to start.

Appliance connectors.

Flexible connectors behind a stove, dryer or water heater can crack with age or after the appliance is moved.

Threaded joints.

Old black iron joints can loosen and seep, especially after settling or vibration.

Shutoff valves.

A worn valve stem can leak at the handle.

Buried lines.

Lines to a grill, pool heater or detached building can corrode or be damaged by digging.

Heating equipment.

A boiler or furnace with a failing gas valve or a cracked heat exchanger can leak gas or carbon monoxide.

Recent work.

A joint disturbed by a remodel or appliance swap can leak if it was not tested.

How a licensed professional finds the leak.

After the building is safe and the utility has cleared it, a licensed professional checks the system methodically. A pressure test holds the line under a known pressure and watches for drops. An electronic combustible gas detector samples the air near joints and fittings, and an approved leak detection solution forms bubbles on a joint that is seeping.

The goal is to find the exact fitting and repair it with the right parts, not to guess. After a repair, the line is tested again before gas is turned back on. Ask for the test result so you know the work was verified.

Carbon monoxide is a separate danger.

A gas leak and carbon monoxide are different hazards. Carbon monoxide has no smell, and it is produced when fuel burns poorly in a furnace, boiler, water heater or stove. A working alarm is the only reliable warning. If it sounds, go outside, call 911 and do not go back in until the building is cleared.

Both hazards are good reasons to have fuel burning equipment inspected before the heating season. A technician checks venting, combustion and connections so small faults do not become emergencies.

Local note.

Stratford note: permits, inspections and licensing.

Gas piping work generally needs a permit and an inspection, and the rules vary by project. Ask the Stratford building department what applies to yours. The Office of the Building Official lists an online permitting portal for applications.

You can confirm a contractor's license through Connecticut's eLicense lookup before they start. Neighbors in Bridgeport and Fairfield can use the same lookup for their own contractors.

According to the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission, carbon monoxide alarms belong near sleeping areas, and local fire officials can advise on placement in your home.

Prevent leaks before they start.

Install a carbon monoxide alarm on each level and near sleeping areas, and test it monthly. Keep the area around gas appliances clear. If you move a stove or dryer, ask a professional to reconnect the gas line and test it. Have heating equipment serviced each year.

If you are planning a new gas appliance, a grill line or a remodel, our plumbing installation page explains how planned work is scheduled. Planned work is different from an emergency, and an emergency always starts with leaving the building.
